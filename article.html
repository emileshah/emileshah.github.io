Why Women-Led Startups Are Still Underfunded — And What's Changing

June 12, 2025 · 8 min read

Despite comprising nearly half the workforce, women-led startups received only 2.3% of venture capital funding last year. The number has barely moved in a decade. Here's what the data actually tells us — and why a new generation of founders and investors is starting to shift the calculus.

The numbers, plainly stated

Every year, the same headline circulates: women-founded startups got a fraction of a percent of VC funding. The story gets picked up, shared, lamented, and then largely forgotten until the next annual report drops. But behind the aggregate statistic is a more textured picture that's worth examining closely.

Funding isn't monolithic. There's pre-seed, seed, Series A, growth, and late stage — and the gap looks different at each level. At the earliest stages, women founders are actually better represented than the 2.3% figure suggests. The problem compounds at growth and late stages, where the largest checks are written and where networks matter most.

"The funding gap isn't just about bias at the individual investor level. It's structural — embedded in how networks form, how pattern-matching happens, and who gets warm introductions."

What the data misses

Annual funding statistics tell us about inputs, not outcomes. What they often obscure is that women-led companies, when they do receive funding, frequently outperform their male-led counterparts on capital efficiency metrics. A 2019 BCG study found that companies founded by women generated 10% more cumulative revenue over a five-year period than companies founded by men, despite receiving substantially less investment.

This isn't a story of talent or capability — it's a story of access. And access is shaped by who knows whom.

The network problem

Most early-stage deals are relationship-driven. A founder gets a warm intro to an investor through a mutual contact — a former colleague, a fellow alum, a co-founder in a shared accelerator cohort. These networks are self-reinforcing: they replicate existing patterns of who has access.

Historically, those networks skewed heavily toward men. Investors who built their careers in the 2000s and 2010s often share backgrounds with male founders from similar universities, industries, and geographies. That's not a conspiracy — it's just how networks form. But the effect is a structural disadvantage for anyone who doesn't fit the historical profile.

What's actually changing

The good news — and there is genuine good news here — is that intentional community-building is starting to work. Programs explicitly designed to connect women founders with investors, mentors, and each other are producing results.

At Berkeley, I've watched this happen firsthand. Events that bring together women founders across industries create the exact kind of serendipitous connection that historically happened organically in homogeneous networks. When you build a room that looks different, different deals get done.

There's also a cohort of investors — many of them women who came up through the industry in the 2010s — who are now writing checks and actively seeking out the deals that established networks miss. That's not charity; that's alpha.

The path forward

Closing the funding gap isn't a single intervention — it's a systems change. It requires investors who are intentional about sourcing, founders who build community rather than compete, and institutions that create structured pathways where organic networks fail.

I'm optimistic. Not because the numbers are great yet — they're not — but because the energy in the community feels different than it did five years ago. The question has shifted from "why does this gap exist?" to "how do we close it?" That's real progress.
